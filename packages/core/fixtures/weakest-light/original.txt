The king had been dead four days when the Order gathered to sing him down.

They did it in the mourning hall, as they had done for every king since the Old Pact. The windows were hung with black cloth, and the only light came from the braziers along the walls, low and red, so that the faces of the assembled mages seemed to float above their dark robes like lanterns on a river. The court stood along the eastern wall in their own mourning colors: ministers, lords of the Council, clerks who would go back to their desks tomorrow and write the old king's name in the past tense for the first time. At their head, nearest the empty bier, stood the Lord Chancellor, Aldric Vane, with his gloved hands folded before him. Everyone in the hall was aware of him without looking. That was the kind of man he was at court. The old king had leaned on him for years, and now the old king was gone, and the court had already begun leaning on him instead.

Bran stood beside Master Hollin in the second rank, where the King's mage and his apprentice belonged, and tried not to think about what came next.

The rite was simple. The rite-master spoke the old king's names, all of them, and at the last name each mage in the hall raised a ward-light to guard his passage. One light for each of them, held steady until the braziers were put out and the hall went dark and there was nothing left in it but the lights themselves. It was meant to be beautiful. It was meant to show the court that the Order stood between the kingdom and whatever waited in the dark.

It was also meant to cost something. Every light did. A ward-light was not a large working, but it drew on the one who made it the way every working did: a little breath, a little warmth, a little of whatever it was that kept a person alive. Old mages went gray early. Some of them went gray at thirty. Bran had grown up in the chapter house among men and women who spent themselves carefully, like misers with coin, and he had learned young that magic was never free. It only let you choose what you paid.

The rite-master reached the last name. The braziers were smothered one by one.

Bran raised his hand with the others and reached for the light.

It came, at first. A small pale glow gathered over his palm, and for a heartbeat he thought it would hold. Then it thinned. It went gray, the color of ash, the color of a sky before sleet, and it guttered like a candle in a draft that no one else could feel. He reached harder. There was nothing to reach for. The light shrank to a point and went out, and he stood in the dark with his hand raised over nothing, and all around him the lights of the Order burned steady and gold.

Beside him, so quickly that he almost missed it, Master Hollin moved two fingers.

A light rose over Bran's palm. It was not his. It was too bright and too still, warmer in color than anything Bran had ever made, and it hung exactly where his own should have been. Hollin did not look at him. His face stayed turned toward the bier, grave and fixed, and only a slight tightening at the corner of his mouth showed what the second light was costing him.

Bran kept his hand raised. He kept his eyes forward. And across the hall, in the half-dark among the court officials, Lord Chancellor Vane was looking at him.

He did not look away when Bran met his eyes. He held the look a long moment, and then he nodded: once, slowly, the way a man might acknowledge a stranger across a crowded street. Bran could not tell what it meant. He could not tell whether Vane had seen the light fail, or seen Hollin's fingers, or seen nothing at all and was only nodding to an apprentice in the second rank because the dead king would have wanted his court to be gracious. The nod ended. Vane turned his face back to the bier.

The lights burned until the rite was done.

#

Hollin waited until they were out of the hall and halfway down the long corridor toward the scriptorium, far enough that the murmur of the court had faded behind them. Then he stopped under one of the narrow windows and turned.

"That's twice this month," he said.

Bran said nothing. There was nothing to say that Hollin didn't already know.

"In front of the whole Order, Bran, on the day we bury a king." Hollin's voice stayed low, but it had an edge in it Bran had rarely heard. "Do you understand what people will say if they see it plainly? Not about you. About me. About whether I've taught you anything at all these twelve years."

Bran opened his mouth.

"Don't. Save it." Hollin rubbed his eyes with the heel of one hand. He looked older than he had that morning, and Bran knew why, and the knowing sat in his stomach like a stone. "You'll go to the vault. Catalogue the relic shelves. Third sublevel, the ones nobody's touched since old Meridic died. It wants doing and it'll keep you out of sight while the court's here mourning. Think of it as a kindness, if you want. It's meant as one."

He began to turn away, then stopped, and when he spoke again the edge had gone out of his voice.

"You're not the weakest mage this Order has ever trained, boy. You're only the one who believes it hardest. Go. I'll send for you when I need you."

The shame came the way it always came, all at once. His face went hot, and the heat of it filled his chest and throat and the backs of his eyes. Hollin meant it kindly, and it changed nothing. He could feel the magic in him as well as anyone; he could feel it moving under his skin on quiet nights. It did not matter that he could feel it. He could not use it the way the others did. It would not answer the same way twice. It came when it chose and left when it chose, and no amount of practice had ever made it reliable, and a mage who could not use what was in him was worse than no mage at all.

"I'm sorry, Master Hollin. I'll do better. I'll—"

Hollin looked at him, and the rest of the promise died in his mouth.

"I'll go to the vault now," Bran said.

"Go on, then. Third sublevel. Take a lamp, not a light-spell. I don't want you spending yourself again today for something a candle can do just as well."

#

The vault lay beneath the chapter house, down a stair so old that its steps had worn into shallow bowls. The first sublevel was kept clean and ordered. The second was kept, at least. The third had been old Meridic's, and Meridic had been dead for six years, and no one had wanted his shelves badly enough to go down and sort them.

Bran set his lamp on the end of the first long table and looked at what he had been given. Shelves ran back into the dark farther than the lamplight reached. They were crowded with boxes and bundles and loose objects under dust thick as felt: broken staves, cracked bowls, rings with no stones in them, bound books that had swollen shut with damp. Some had tags in Meridic's small, cramped hand. Most had nothing.

He found the ledger where Hollin had said it would be, and a pen, and ink that had not quite dried out. He began at the nearest shelf.

It was slow work and he did not mind it. There was a kind of peace in it: lift the thing, look at it, turn it to the light, write down what it was and where it had been and whether any working still lived in it. Most did not. Most were only objects now, the magic long since spent out of them. He wrote *inert* beside them one after another in a neat column and let the quiet close over him. If he could not hold a ward-light, he could at least do this well. He could leave the shelves in better order than he found them, and perhaps someone would notice, and perhaps it would count for something. It was a small hope. It was the size of hope he could afford.

He had worked his way a third of the way down the second row when he found the wardstone.

It lay in a shallow box lined with old cloth, and it was not much to look at: a fragment of gray stone the size of his palm, cracked straight through, its edges rough where it had broken from something larger. Two tags were tied to the box. The older was brittle parchment, its ink faded almost to nothing, the letters in a hand Bran did not know. The newer was in Meridic's hand, copied out years ago beside it, as though the old man had feared the first would crumble: *Threshold. North face. Recovered after the slide.* Bran read it twice. Mount Threshold. The mountain where the lone mage lived. The mountain every king went to once and never spoke of after.

He lifted the stone out of its box.

It was warm.

Not warm the way a thing gets from lying near a lamp. Warm from inside, as though something in it were alive and breathing slowly. And under the warmth, so faint he felt it in his fingers before he heard it, there was a hum. It moved up through his hand and into his wrist and settled somewhere beneath his ribs, and as it settled, the crack in the stone seemed to darken. For a moment, in the lamplight, the dark in the crack was not gray at all but the color of an old bruise, purple going black.

Bran put the stone down very quickly.

He stood looking at it with his hand held a little away from his body, as if it had been burned. The hum did not stop when he let go. It was fainter, but it was there, low in his chest, like the last note of a bell after the bell has been struck.

He should tell someone. That was the plain truth of it. A relic from Mount Threshold that still held a working was not a thing an apprentice ought to find alone in a cellar and keep to himself. He should go upstairs and find Hollin and tell him.

And say what? That the only mage in the chapter house who could not hold a ward-light had felt something no one else had felt in six years? That a stone had hummed at him? Hollin had already spent himself once that day covering for Bran's failures. Bran could imagine his face.

He picked up the pen. He wrote the stone's description in the ledger, and its shelf and row, and the words from Meridic's tag. Then, in the column beside it, in the same neat hand as all the others, he wrote *inert*.

He put the lid on the box and put the box back on the shelf.

#

He kept working, row after row, until the oil in his lamp ran low, because he had been told to finish and because stopping would have meant thinking.

It did not work. He thought anyway. He lifted cracked bowls and rusted clasps and wrote them down, and all the while his mind circled the stone the way a dog circles something it has buried. He told himself it had been the lamp. Old stone held heat. He had been tired, and ashamed, and he had wanted something to happen to him that was not failure, and so he had felt something happen. That was all. Minds did that.

But he remembered the warmth coming from the inside out. He remembered the color in the crack. He remembered the way the hum had found its place beneath his ribs as if it had known exactly where to go, as if there had been a space there waiting for it. None of the magic he had ever made had felt like that. His own magic slipped away from him; this had come *toward* him. And every time he pushed the thought away another came behind it. Why a stone from the mountain. Why it was here, in a box, in the dark. Whether Meridic had felt it too. Whether anyone had. Whether it meant something was wrong with him, some new flaw on top of the old ones.

Whether it meant something else.

He did not let himself finish that thought.

It was late when he climbed the worn stair with the ledger under his arm. The duty-clerk in the upper hall took it without looking up, marked it received, and set it on a pile of other ledgers. Bran stood there a moment longer than he needed to. The clerk glanced at him. Bran said goodnight and went to his room.

He lay on his narrow bed in the dark and listened to the chapter house settle around him. The hum was still there. Very faint now. He could have mistaken it for his own pulse, if he had wanted to.

He wanted to. He tried.

#

He dreamed of shelves.

They went on forever, row after row into a dark that had no back wall, and he walked between them with a lamp that gave no light. Every object he passed was wrapped in cloth, and every one of them was warm. Somewhere far ahead a crack of bruise-colored light lay open on the air like a wound, pulsing slowly, and he could not tell whether he was walking toward it or it was coming toward him.

Something called his name. There was no voice in it. It was only the shape of his name, pressed against him from all sides, the way cold presses.

Then he was in the mourning hall, and the braziers were going out, and he raised his hand. The light came gray and thin and died, and died again, and died again, and each time Hollin's fingers moved at the edge of his sight and another light rose that was not his. Hollin did not look at him. No one looked at him. The bruise-colored crack hung above the bier where the old king lay, and it was very patient.

He woke twice in the night with his sheets twisted around his legs, not knowing where he was. By morning he remembered almost none of it. Only the shelves, and the patience, and the feeling of having been called.

#

The courtyard was cold and bright, and Bran walked it three times before he admitted that walking was not helping.

The chapter house was full of court. Lords and ministers who had come for the rite still lingered in the colonnades, talking in low voices, and their servants carried messages back and forth across the yard. Bran kept to the edges. He stopped by the old well and looked down into it without seeing anything, and he was still standing there when a voice spoke behind him.

He turned, and straightened, and found himself face to face with the Lord Chancellor.

Aldric Vane was not a tall man, but he held himself as though he were. His mourning clothes were very fine and very plain. He wore dark gloves, and his sleeves came low over them, and he looked at Bran with an expression of such careful warmth that Bran did not know what to do with his hands.

"You needn't stand at attention, Master Greyholdt. I've no rank in your Order." Vane's voice was quiet and pleasant. "The Order's rolls say you were taken in as a child." Bran felt the heat climb into his face and kept his eyes on the Chancellor's collar.

"I saw your light fail yesterday," Vane went on. Bran's hands went cold. The light that failed, then, and not only the one that had risen after it; and if Vane had seen that much, he might have seen Hollin's fingers too. Bran could not ask. He could not even let it show.

"I was an orphan too. Goblins came down on my father's house one autumn. By morning there was no one left who knew my name." He said it gently, the way a man offers something of his own so another need not feel alone with his. "The peace was meant to prevent that. It didn't." He tilted his head slightly. "Does Hollin tell you why your light failed?"

"No, my lord," Bran said. Then, because that was hardly an answer: "Master Hollin says it's only practice. That it'll come, if I work hard enough." Vane waited, and the silence drew the rest out of him. "He doesn't say why. I don't think anyone knows why. Not for certain."

Vane nodded as if this were a thoughtful thing to have said. As he did, he lifted one hand to settle his cloak, and the glove, loose at the cuff, slipped down across his knuckles. Beneath it a bandage was wound flat across the whole of his palm. It was fresh; the linen was still white at its edges. But at its center, where it crossed the heel of his hand, it was spotted dark with blood that had soaked through and not yet dried.

The glove slid back into place as he lowered his hand. Vane did not seem to pay much attention to it.

Bran looked at the other glove, the other sleeve. At the way Vane held both hands, a little stiffly, a little close. When Vane shifted his weight, the cuff of the second glove gaped for a moment, and Bran saw the same clean white linen there also, wrapped across that palm too. Both hands, then.

"My lord," he said, before he could think better of it. "Your hands. Do you need help with them? The infirmary here is good. I could take you."

For a moment something moved behind Vane's eyes. Then it was gone, and he smiled.

"An old injury, nothing more. The price of a life spent signing papers with a temper." He let the smile widen a little. "You're kind to ask. Most at court learn not to notice such things, or pretend not to. But you, I think, notice a great deal more than they credit you for."

Bran did not know what to say to that. He did not like the way it made him feel, warm and exposed at the same time, like standing too near a fire in a room where the door has been left open. And beneath the warmth sat a small, plain thought he could not put down: an old injury did not bleed through new linen. He had seen the blood not a minute since, still wet at the heel of the hand. He kept the thought to himself.

"Tell me instead what practice feels like, when it fails you," Vane said. "Not what Hollin says. What you feel, in the moment the light dies."

Bran wanted very much to be somewhere else. He did not know why. Vane had said nothing unkind; if anything he had been kinder than anyone at the chapter house had been to Bran in months. But the question sat wrong, and the blood on the bandage sat wrong, and Bran had the clear sense of a conversation walking toward a place he did not want it to go. He could not simply leave. One did not simply leave the Lord Chancellor standing in a courtyard. He would have to answer, briefly, and politely, and then find some reason to excuse himself.

"It feels like reaching for a rope that isn't—"

The bell rang out across the courtyard and swallowed the rest.

It was the assembly bell, and it was ringing fast, not the slow measured stroke of the hours. All across the yard people stopped what they were doing and turned toward the chapter house doors.

Vane stepped back. "Go on, they're calling you. We'll finish this another time, Master Greyholdt." He paused, and one gloved hand rested lightly, briefly, against the palm of the other.

"Apprentice!" Hollin's voice cut across the courtyard from the steps. "Assembly, now, not chatting in the yard like it's market day. Move."

Bran moved.

#

The great hall was crowded to its walls. Every mage in the chapter house was there, and most of the lingering court besides, and near the back, apart from both, stood a tall figure in gray-green whom Bran knew only by rumor: the elven emissary, Ithuriel Vann, sent from the Hallow Wood to watch the human court at its weakest. He stood very still and watched everything.

The rite-master climbed the dais and waited until the hall went quiet.

"Word has come from the palace," he said. "The Council has confirmed the nomination. Prince Edmund will be crowned upon returning from Mount Threshold."

A murmur passed through the hall and died.

"As the Old Pact requires, the new king's delegation will travel to Mount Threshold before the coronation is sealed, to stand once before the lone mage, as every king before him has. Master Hollin will lead the Order's party."

Bran felt the hum stir beneath his ribs at the name of the mountain. He pressed his hand flat against his chest without meaning to, and then took it away.

Hollin found him afterward in the side passage, where the crowd thinned out.

"You heard the assembly," he said. "The delegation leaves within the week." He looked at Bran for a long moment. "You're coming with me, Bran. To the mountain. To stand before Ossian Thray."

Bran stared at him.

"I won't pretend it's an honor," Hollin said. "No apprentice has ever gone before. But I won't leave you here with the court hunting reasons to doubt the Order. You stay close to me on that mountain. You speak when spoken to, and otherwise you keep so quiet that no one remembers you came at all."

Bran heard himself say that he understood. He did not. He thought of the lone mage, the man the three kings feared, a man Bran knew only from stories told low in the chapter house: that he had held the mountain alone for longer than most at court had been alive, that the wind did not touch him. He thought of the stone in its box three floors below his feet.

"Why?" he said. "Why me, Master?"

Hollin's mouth thinned.

"Because it isn't safe to leave you behind." He held up a hand as Bran drew breath to answer. "You're my apprentice. You can't protect yourself yet, so I will."

Bran's mouth went dry, and something tightened low in his gut and would not loosen.

Hollin glanced back along the passage, toward the hall where the court still lingered.

He stepped closer and lowered his voice further.

"So you're coming where I can see you. I'd rather that than wonder about what is happening back here with a boy who can't hold a ward-light. Do you understand me now?"

"Yes, Master."

"Pack light. We leave in five days, and the mountain doesn't forgive anyone who arrives unprepared. Not kings. Not mages."

#

The five days went quickly, and they went slowly.

Bran packed and repacked. Wool, and more wool. Boots he greased twice. A knife, flint, a small kit of salves from the infirmary. Hollin gave him a list and he followed it exactly, and then he lay awake at night and went through it again in his head.

He did not know what was expected of an apprentice in a king's delegation, or whether anything was expected of him at all.

He saw little of Hollin. The King's mage was everywhere those days, in council, in the palace, in the chapter house offices with the door shut, and when Bran did see him he was curt and tired and thinking about something else. On the third day Bran knocked and went in with a question about the list, and found Hollin at his desk with one of the senior mages bent close beside him and a letter open on the wood between them. They stopped talking at once. The other mage folded the letter in half, and in half again, and slid it away inside a sleeve, and Hollin asked what Bran wanted in a voice that made it plain he should want it quickly. Bran asked his question and left, and the murmur started again before the door had closed behind him. It was not the last time. Twice more he came upon Hollin in a passage with one senior mage or another, heads together, and each time the talk stopped when they saw him. After that Bran did not trouble him. He did not tell him what Vane had said by the well, that he had seen the light fail; Hollin would ask what else the Chancellor had seen, and Bran had no answer to give him. He did not mention the wardstone either. Each day it seemed more foolish to, and each night the hum beneath his ribs seemed no fainter than the night before.

On the fifth morning he carried his pack down to the chapter house gate before dawn. The air was cold enough to hurt. Horses stamped in the yard beyond, and the delegation was gathering in the gray light, and Hollin stood by the gate in his traveling cloak with his breath smoking.

He looked Bran over from boots to hood. Whatever he was looking for, he seemed to find enough of it.

"Ready as you'll be," he said.
